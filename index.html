The discipline

What we mean by an ontology

Every organisation has people who can answer the hard questions. They know which report is wrong, which column in the database actually matters, why two departments use different words for the same thing, and how an approval really works.

Write that knowledge down properly and you have an ontology. At the moment it sits in a few people's heads.

We write it down for whatever system you already run, in whatever field you work in. Our software reads your systems and drafts the first version, working out what each thing means, how things connect, and who decides. We set the rules it follows and check what it produces.

After that your staff and your AI can both ask it questions, and every answer shows the record it came from.

Why it repeats

The same software does all of it

Five government portals. An archive of scanned documents in five languages. A library of more than 2,000 works spanning twenty-five centuries. A publisher's research and production line. Every one of them stored its data differently, and no two were in the same business.

On one government portal it made 115,652 decisions about what the data meant.

In a historical archive it read more than 1,000 works and recorded who was mentioned in each one, on what subject and in what context.

For a publisher it has drafted hundreds of illustrated books on its own, researching each one first and citing a real source on every line.

Built on top of it The Atlas

A map of the collection drawn from the texts themselves: the people, ideas, places, groups and events across twenty-five centuries, and every time one text cites another, including whether the later author agreed or argued back.

The rule underneath It cannot invent a quotation

The software that builds the Atlas is not allowed to write quotations. It can only point at paragraphs, and the words are attached afterwards from the source text.

An AI startup · Data ontology

Hard problems can be solved when people understand their data. Thothica helps them do that by building ontologies and AI-native software.

Every organization has people who know how to answer the difficult questions. They know which reports are wrong, which database column actually matters, why two departments use different names for the same thing, and how an approval really works.

An ontology is that knowledge, written down precisely enough that software can run on it. Today it lives in a few people's heads.

Thothica writes it down for whatever system you already run, in whatever field you work in.

Our software reads your systems and writes the first version of the ontology. It works out what each item means, how the items relate to one another, and who decides. We set the rules it follows and check its work. Then your staff and your AI can both ask it questions, and every answer shows which record it came from.

Your organization no longer depends on only a few experienced people to interpret its data. You can solve, at scale, the problems those people used to solve one at a time.

Logic sources

What you know

  • Formulas buried in spreadsheets
  • Existing code
  • Machine learning models
  • Written rules and prompts

Some of it is in no system at all. It sits with the people who have done the job for years. We send in forward deployed agents: software that works alongside your experts, asks them how the job is really done, and writes the answers down in the same structure as everything else.

Systems of action

What you do

  • Your ERP

We build the system you act in, or we put the layer inside the one you already use, so a decision arrives as a step someone can take.

Together, these three are the ontology. Written down in one place, in one structure, so your people and your software read the same thing.

Case studies

  1. 1

    We built the AI layer that now runs on five state government portals, each built on a different technology. For each portal we first wrote down what its data measures and the ways it can be broken down: by district, by scheme, by month, by status.

    An official can then ask a question in plain language and get an answer taken from that portal's own records, with every figure traceable back to the rows it came from. The layer only reads. It cannot change anything in the system it sits on.

  2. 2

    For a think tank, we rebuilt an archive of more than 1,000 historical works. What they had was more than 2,000 PDFs, most of them scans of printed pages, sitting on a slow, outdated website. A scan is a picture, so none of it could be searched. Our software turned those pictures into text that people can read and machines can use.

    The ontology was the real work. Our software identified every writer in the collection and matched their names across scripts and spellings, so that one person stays one person however a byline was printed. It recorded what kind of document each item is, whether a speech, a pamphlet, a book or a periodical article, and which subjects each writer wrote on.

    It then read the works themselves and recorded who was mentioned in each one, on what subject, and in what context. Where a person mentioned in one work also wrote works of their own, the two are joined up. So you can follow who wrote about whom, who replied to whom, and what was actually said. Every work also carries a summary and its main arguments, taken from the text and pointing back to it.

    On top of that we built a content management system designed around this structure, and a fast digital library. Researchers can search it in five languages, and every answer points back to the original document.

  3. 3

    For one of India's largest publishers, we built a production line for research-based graphic stories. We first turn the source material, which may be books, long interviews or video, into a structured research library where every chapter is indexed and every fact keeps a record of where it came from.

    Scripts are drafted from that library, and every line carries a citation back to the exact source line. A check resolves each citation before the script goes forward, so nothing unsourced reaches the page. Our software then keeps the characters and the drawing style consistent from page to page, through to print-ready files. It has drafted hundreds of comic books this way.

Use cases

  1. 1

    We integrate AI layers into legacy software. Your existing system stays exactly as it is. We read its database and write down what its tables and columns mean in the words your staff actually use, and the layer sits on top of that.

    Your staff can then ask questions in plain language instead of opening many screens and exporting spreadsheets. Because the layer answers from a written record rather than guessing, every number it reports can be traced back to the data it came from.

  2. 2

    We build approval systems for governments. Before we write any software, we write down what each scheme actually requires: which document proves which condition, which officer signs at which stage, and what makes an application complete.

    The software then checks every application against that record. The applicant sees exactly what is missing. The official sees which files are ready for a decision, and which are stuck and why. Approvals move faster because nobody has to work out what a case needs.

Open source

  1. 1

    We run Falsafa.ai, our open-source library of the world's philosophical, classical and religious texts. It holds more than 2,000 works, divided into more than 1.3 million passages that can each be quoted and cited exactly.

    On top of the library we build the Atlas, a map of the collection drawn from the texts themselves. It records the people, ideas, places, groups, events and objects that appear across twenty-five centuries of writing. It also records every time one text cites another, and how: whether the later author treated the earlier one as an authority, agreed with it, extended it, or argued against it.

    Every entry is tied to a paragraph you can read in place. The software that builds the Atlas is not allowed to write quotations. It can only point at paragraphs, and the words are attached afterwards from the source text, so an invented quotation is impossible.

The same software does all of it. On one government portal it made 115,652 decisions about what the data meant. In a historical archive it read more than 1,000 works and recorded who was mentioned in each one, on what subject and in what context. For a publisher it has drafted hundreds of comic books on its own, researching each one first and citing a real source on every line.

Five government portals. An archive of scanned documents in five languages. A library of more than 2,000 works spanning twenty-five centuries. A publisher's research and production line. Every one of them stored its data differently, and no two were in the same business. Our way of working did not change once. There is more running now that we have not announced.
